How to take meeting notes that actually get used

Most meeting notes get written and never read again. Here's a structure that people actually open afterward, because it tells them exactly what they're supposed to do next.

The short version: write down what was decided, what needs to happen next, who's doing it, and by when. Skip the rest. Send it within an hour, to everyone who was in the room, in a format short enough that people actually read it.

What to write down (and what to skip)

A meeting produces a lot of talk and very little of it needs to survive in your notes. If you try to capture everything, you end up typing instead of listening, and the note is too long for anyone to read later. Aim for four categories:

  • Decisions. Anything the group agreed on, chose between, or ruled out. "We're launching the orange version first" is worth writing down. The five minutes of debate that led there usually isn't.
  • Action items. Anything someone is now supposed to go do. Write it as a task: "Send the revised budget to finance" rather than "we talked about the budget."
  • Owners. Every action item needs exactly one name attached. "The team will follow up" quietly means nobody will. If two people need to work on something, pick one as the owner and let the other be support.
  • Dates. "Soon" is not a date. If nobody stated one out loud, ask before the meeting ends, or write "no date set, follow up" so it doesn't silently vanish.

Everything else, the side conversations, the jokes, the three ways someone explained the same idea, can be left out. If it becomes important later, someone will bring it up again.

Simple methods that work

You don't need a system with a name. Three approaches cover almost every meeting:

  • The running list. One column, one line per decision or action item, written in the order they come up. Fastest to write, and enough for a short check-in or status call.
  • The agenda-as-notes. Before the meeting, write the agenda topics as headings. During the meeting, add a line or two under each one. This works well when a meeting has more than two topics, since it keeps notes organized by subject instead of by time.
  • The two-column split. Discussion on the left, decisions and action items on the right. Useful in longer or more technical meetings where you want a record of the reasoning, but still want the action items to stand out at a glance.

Pick one before the meeting starts. Switching methods mid-meeting is how notes turn into a mess.

A copy-paste note structure

This is the format to start from. Paste it into a blank document before your next meeting and fill in the blanks as you go.

Meeting: [name] Date: [date] Attendees: [names] Summary (1-2 sentences) Decisions - Action items - [task] | owner: [name] | due: [date] - [task] | owner: [name] | due: [date] Open questions / follow-up needed -

The summary line matters more than it looks. A single sentence at the top ("Agreed to push the launch two weeks and split QA between Sam and Priya") lets someone who missed the meeting understand the outcome without reading the rest.

After the meeting: the ten-minute habit

Notes taken during a meeting are usually rough, half-sentences, abbreviations, things only you would understand. The habit that separates useful notes from a pile of scribbles is a short cleanup pass right after:

  1. Do it right away. Ten minutes immediately after the call beats thirty minutes tomorrow, because you still remember what your shorthand meant.
  2. Turn fragments into full sentences. "Sam - api - fri" becomes "Sam to finish the API integration by Friday." Future-you, and everyone else reading it, needs the second version.
  3. Send it to the room. A note nobody sees might as well not exist. Email or message it to everyone who attended, plus anyone who needed to be there but couldn't make it.
  4. Put action items where you'll actually see them. A note buried in a folder doesn't remind anyone of anything. Copy your action items into whatever list you check daily, a task app, a calendar, even a sticky note, so "due Friday" has a chance of happening on Friday.

Questions people ask

What should you actually write down in a meeting?

Four things: decisions that were made, tasks someone now owns, who owns each task, and any date attached to it. Most of what's said in a meeting is discussion that leads up to a decision. Write down where the discussion landed and skip the path it took to get there.

Should you type notes or write them by hand?

Whichever one you'll actually finish and send afterward. Handwriting is often more memorable and less distracting on a video call, but typed notes are faster to turn into a follow-up message. If you type, keep your notes app separate from anything with notifications, so you're not half in the meeting and half in email.

How long should meeting notes be?

Short enough that someone reads all of it. A meeting note with a two-line summary, a short decisions list, and an action item table usually beats a page of prose, because the reader can scan it in fifteen seconds and find what applies to them.

Do I need special software to take good meeting notes?

No. A blank document and the structure on this page is enough. Software helps if you'd rather not type while you're trying to listen, since an app can capture the conversation and hand you a draft to edit instead of a blank page.
