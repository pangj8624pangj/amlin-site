How meeting notes work when no bot joins the call

Most AI note apps send a bot into your video call. A few skip that and listen from your own computer instead. This page explains how the second kind works, what it changes for the people on your call, and where it falls short.

The short version: a no-bot app records nothing inside the video call. It runs on your own computer, listens to the sound that computer is already playing plus your microphone, and turns that sound into text as the meeting happens. Because it never joins as a participant, the other people on the call see no extra attendee and install nothing. It only hears what your one computer hears.

What is a meeting bot?

A meeting bot is a piece of software that signs in to your Zoom, Google Meet, or Microsoft Teams call as if it were a guest. It appears in the participant list, often under a name like "Sam's Notetaker." The host may have to admit it from the waiting room. Once inside, it receives the same audio and video stream as every other attendee, sends it to the company's servers, and comes back with a transcript and summary.

That design has one big advantage. The bot lives in the cloud, so it can attend a meeting even if your laptop is closed. It also has a cost. Everyone on the call can see a recorder is present, some organizations block unfamiliar bots outright, and a copy of the recording usually sits on the vendor's servers afterward.

How can an app take notes without joining the call?

Think about what happens on your Mac during a video call. The other people's voices come out of your speakers or headphones. Your own voice goes in through your microphone. A no-bot app listens at those two points on your machine. The call itself is untouched, and the video service has no idea anything else is paying attention.

On a Mac, Apple provides a way for an app to capture the sound the computer is playing, and macOS asks you for permission the first time. That is why these apps work with Zoom, Meet, Teams, and anything else that makes noise, including a phone call on speaker. There is no list of supported video services to check, because the app never talks to any of them.

What changes for the other people on the call?

  • They see no extra attendee in the participant list.
  • They do not need to install, approve, or sign in to anything.
  • The host does not have to admit a guest or change any meeting setting.
  • Nothing tells them notes are being taken, so telling them is up to you.

That last point deserves attention. A visible bot doubles as an announcement that the call is being recorded. Without one, you carry the job of saying so. Laws on recording and transcribing conversations differ by country and by state, and some places require everyone's agreement first. A short sentence at the start of a call, such as "I'm taking notes with an app on my computer, is that okay?", covers you in most situations, and it is good manners too.

What do you give up with no-bot capture?

There are three limits worth knowing before you choose.

  1. Your computer has to be there. A bot can attend while your laptop is shut. A no-bot app needs to be running on the machine that is hearing the call.
  2. One vantage point. It hears what your Mac hears. A meeting you join from your phone will not reach it.
  3. Usually no video to replay. Bots often keep the video so you can watch it later. An app that only listens from your computer may keep a transcript and nothing else.

Who is each approach a good fit for?

A bot suits recurring internal meetings where everybody expects a recorder, and where a shared library of recordings is something the team wants. No-bot capture suits conversations where an extra attendee would change the mood: a client call, a candidate interview, a sensitive one-on-one, or a meeting on a customer's own video account where you cannot bring a guest. Some people use both, depending on the meeting.

What happens to the audio afterward?

The answer depends on the app, and skipping the bot does not settle it. An app can still upload the audio and store it. Ask three things before you trust any of them with a serious conversation: is the audio saved, for how long, and who besides you can open it.

Questions people ask

Can an app take meeting notes without a bot joining the call?

Yes. An app running on your own computer can pick up the sound the computer plays, which is the other participants' voices, plus your microphone. It turns that sound into text as the meeting happens. Nothing joins the call, so nobody else sees an extra attendee or has to approve anything.

Does the other person know I'm taking notes?

Not automatically. With no bot in the call, nothing on the other person's screen changes. That makes telling them your responsibility. Depending on where you and they are located, you may need their consent before recording or transcribing a conversation, so check the rules that apply to you and ask when in doubt.

Does no-bot capture work for phone calls and in-person meetings?

It works for anything your Mac can hear. A video call, a call on speaker, or a conversation in a room picked up by the microphone are all possible. It cannot capture a call you take on a different device, because the sound has to reach the computer running the app.

What are the downsides of no-bot capture?

It only hears what happens on the one computer it runs on, so it cannot join a call on your behalf while you are away, and it produces no video file to replay. If you want a recording to watch later, a bot-based tool is the better fit.

Is audio stored when no bot is used?

That depends on the app. Some upload and keep the audio, others transcribe it and discard it. Amlin transcribes live and never stores the meeting audio, so only the text remains.
